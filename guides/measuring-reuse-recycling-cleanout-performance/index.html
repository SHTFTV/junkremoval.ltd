Professionals / Market and measurement

Measure cleanout performance without inflating the diversion story

Build useful reuse, recycling, residual and service metrics from job-level evidence, with clear boundaries and honest uncertainty.

The working brief

  • Define the denominator and period before reporting a rate.
  • Separate measured quantities from allocations and estimates.
  • Use official market data as context, not proof of company outcomes.

Choose the decision the measure should improve

A removal operator, property manager and public policymaker may all discuss waste performance while asking different questions. An operator needs to understand routing, cost and service reliability. A property manager may need evidence for a specific clearance. A policymaker may be comparing material flows across an economy. Start by naming the decision and audience before choosing a metric. Otherwise a convenient number can travel from one context into another and acquire a meaning the evidence never supported.

Write a short measurement brief. State which jobs, sites, materials and dates are included; which outcomes matter; and which records are available. Identify the person who owns the definitions and the person who checks the results. A small project can do this in a few paragraphs. The important point is consistency. If one month counts furniture offered for donation and the next counts only accepted deliveries, an apparent performance change may be a definition change rather than a change in the work.

Separate prevention, reuse, recycling and residual routes

Prevention means an unwanted material was not generated in the first place. Reuse keeps an item useful, while recycling involves a material-processing route. Energy recovery and residual disposal are other outcomes that should have their own labels where relevant. Do not compress all favourable-sounding activities into one category called recycled. An office desk transferred intact to another workplace tells a different story from a desk dismantled for material recovery.

For cleanout reporting, record the action the evidence actually demonstrates. An item retained on site can be shown as retained, but it may sit outside a removal contractor’s collected-load denominator. A batch accepted by a reuse organisation can be reported as accepted for reuse with the relevant qualification. A delivery to a recycler demonstrates that delivery; detailed downstream claims need additional evidence. Distinguishing these stages may make the report less dramatic, but it makes it more useful to the next person who must decide how to improve the process.

Define the reporting boundary before calculating a rate

A diversion percentage has a numerator and a denominator, and both require definitions. Does the denominator include all material in the original property inventory, only material collected by one contractor or only material with measured weights? Are hazardous streams reported separately? Does the period follow collection date or final destination date? Write those choices alongside the result. A percentage without a boundary invites readers to assume a broader claim than the project can substantiate.

Keep project-level and company-level reporting separate. A single unusually metal-heavy job can produce a high recycling share by weight without reflecting the ordinary work mix. A company total can conceal a particular customer’s rejected reuse batch. Use the right scale for the question and explain exclusions. If a customer asks for a project result that the records cannot support, provide the available counts and destination evidence instead of borrowing the operator’s overall percentage. An honest partial result is preferable to a precise but unrelated figure.

Build the evidence ladder into the data

Assign an evidence type to each quantity and outcome. Direct measured weight, receiving item count, crew estimate and modelled allocation are different data qualities. A photograph may support the presence or condition of items but is rarely a reliable measurement of a mixed load’s mass. Preserve the original receipt or ticket and link the reported row to it. That connection allows someone else to reproduce the result without depending on the report author’s memory.

A practical dataset can include job identifier, stream, unit, quantity, measurement method, destination, acceptance status, evidence reference and unresolved notes. Keep the raw record separate from later calculations. If a receiving facility revises a ticket or the customer retains an item at the last moment, update the reported dataset through a controlled correction. Do not erase the original event. Measurement systems become trustworthy through the ability to explain discrepancies, not through the absence of visible corrections.

Keep units compatible and estimates visible

Item counts can be excellent for furniture reuse. Weight can be useful for material flows. Volume can help operational planning. None is a universal substitute for the others. If the project counts twenty chairs and measures a residual load in kilograms, report both unless a defensible conversion method is needed for a specific purpose. Do not add the values together or calculate a percentage across unlike units. A clear table with two measures is better than a single synthetic score.

When estimating weight from item information, state the source of the assumptions and apply them consistently. Avoid presenting the estimate with a level of precision that implies a scale measurement. Mixed-load allocations require particular care. A truck ticket covering several customers cannot become a direct measured weight for every customer. If an allocation is used, explain the basis and its limitations. Where that uncertainty is too large for the intended claim, keep the result qualitative or provide a range with a clear explanation.

Distinguish route choice from final material outcome

An operator often controls collection and delivery but has limited visibility after the receiving gate. A delivery to a sorting facility can be accurately recorded without claiming that every kilogram was recycled. Ask the facility what documentation it provides and what its reported processing figures mean. A facility-wide rate may describe a particular period and mix of inputs; it is not automatically a measured result for one customer’s load. Keep those distinctions in the report wording.

The same issue appears in reuse. A recipient’s acceptance is meaningful evidence that the transfer occurred, but it does not prove how long the item remained in use. If the recipient later confirms placement, report that additional evidence separately. Avoid making an unqualified “saved from landfill” claim solely because an item was offered online or put in storage. A staged outcome model—offered, accepted, delivered, confirmed in use—can support improvement without pretending every stage is the final result.

Use official market data for context, not self-certification

Statistics Canada’s waste-management publications provide national and regional context on disposal and diversion. Its survey methodology identifies the activities and organisations included in the collection. That context can help a reader understand the wider system, but it cannot certify an individual removal company’s performance. A national material-flow statistic has a different boundary from a household cleanout and should not be used as a benchmark without checking definitions, period and source coverage.

When writing a market article, cite the exact release or table used and distinguish publication date from the period measured. Check whether a series has changed frequency or methodology before comparing points. Prefer the original statistical source to a secondary headline. This guide intentionally avoids a current market-size or growth figure because such a claim needs a specific, dated analysis. Useful market reporting can explain what the data measures, what it omits and which operational questions remain local, even when it does not produce an impressive forecast.

Measure service performance alongside material outcomes

A reuse route that repeatedly misses the property handover deadline may not be viable without changes. Track service measures such as confirmed arrival windows met, jobs completed to authorised scope, rejected deliveries, unresolved exceptions and evidence returned on time. Define each measure so that staff can apply it consistently. Separate customer-requested changes from operational failures, while still examining whether earlier questions could have prevented the surprise. The goal is to improve the process, not to build a blame table.

Pair rates with counts. One rejected delivery out of a small number of jobs can create a dramatic percentage that needs context. Review the reasons behind the result: inaccurate descriptions, recipient capacity, damaged items or access problems suggest different actions. Material and service measures together can reveal whether earlier surveying or better batch information would improve both reuse acceptance and customer reliability. A narrow focus on diversion alone can hide the costs and delays that eventually undermine a promising route.

Use financial measures without misleading comparisons

A cleanout’s cost depends on labour, travel, access, equipment, receiving routes and the material mix. Compare like work when assessing efficiency. A difficult upper-floor furniture removal is not directly comparable to a ground-level container exchange. Record the relevant job characteristics and review groups with similar conditions. Otherwise a dashboard may reward taking easy work and penalise staff who manage complex sites well. Financial analysis should explain the operation rather than flatten it into a single average.

If evaluating reuse, include the effort required to inspect, photograph, dismantle, protect and deliver accepted items, as well as any avoided costs or proceeds that can actually be documented. Do not assume donated furniture has a resale value available to the project. A recipient’s social value is also not a cash saving to the donor. Keep financial, material and social outcomes distinct, with methods appropriate to each. The organisation can then make a reasoned decision about what it is willing to fund and what evidence it expects.

Avoid unsupported carbon claims

Carbon estimates require a method with defined assumptions, boundaries and material pathways. A weight delivered to a reuse organisation does not translate automatically into a precise emissions saving. The calculation may depend on what product is displaced, how long an item remains useful, transport, processing and the chosen reference scenario. A removal operator should not invent a universal factor to turn every collected tonne into a promotional climate number.

If a customer needs an emissions assessment, identify an appropriate recognised method and a competent person to apply it to the available data. Keep the measured activity data and the modelled environmental result separate. Report assumptions, exclusions and uncertainty in a form the reader can understand. If those requirements cannot be met, communicate the verified material actions instead. Saying that a defined batch was accepted for reuse is useful and concrete. It does not become more credible by adding a carbon figure that nobody can reproduce.

Create a review routine that leads to action

Review a manageable set of measures at a regular operational meeting. Start with missing or inconsistent evidence, then examine the largest causes of rejected loads, additional visits or unplanned residual disposal. Choose a small number of actions with named owners. Examples might include improving intake photographs, confirming recipient acceptance earlier or separating a stream before loading. Check the next period to see whether the change worked. A dashboard without a decision routine is an archive of colourful numbers.

Use exceptions as learning material. A low-reuse job may have been appropriately managed because the contents were damaged or unsuitable. A high-recycling job may have required little operational skill because it consisted mainly of a readily accepted metal stream. Review the facts before drawing conclusions. Share definitions with customers and staff so that everyone understands what is being measured. This reduces pressure to improve results by changing labels rather than changing the underlying work.

Use a worked example without turning it into a benchmark

Consider a hypothetical office project that inventories ten desks. Two stay in the organisation, six are accepted and delivered to a reuse recipient, and two go through another documented route. An item-count report can describe those outcomes directly. If the removal contractor collected only eight desks, its collected-item denominator differs from the property manager’s ten-item inventory. Neither boundary is inherently dishonest, but the report must say which one it uses.

Now suppose the residual route also includes unrelated mixed material weighed on a shared vehicle. That ticket does not establish the weight of the two desks alone. The report should preserve the item count and label any allocation separately. This example supplies no industry target and no claim about a real job. Its purpose is to show how a simple result changes meaning when the scope or unit changes. A transparent explanation can prevent a plausible-looking percentage from becoming a misleading public claim.

Publish a result that another person can check

A credible public result includes the period, scope, units, outcome definitions and evidence basis. If the report combines measured and estimated data, say so near the result. Explain major exclusions and unresolved quantities. Avoid claiming complete traceability when some downstream stages remain unknown. A concise methodology note can make a short report far more trustworthy than a long narrative filled with unqualified environmental language.

The reporting system should also preserve a practical route back to the source records for authorised reviewers. That does not mean publishing customer addresses or private receipts online. It means maintaining an internal audit trail and sharing appropriate evidence through a controlled process. Good measurement helps customers choose, operators improve and editors explain the market. Its value comes from consistent definitions and honest limits. The strongest performance story is the one that remains understandable when someone asks exactly what was counted, where it went and how the conclusion was reached.
